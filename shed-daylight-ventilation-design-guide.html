Design Guide

Daylight and airflow: let the light in and the heat out

Insulation slows heat getting in. Ventilation and roof lights decide what happens to the heat and light already inside. See how air moves through a shed and size both for your floor area.

November 202610 min readBy R.R Teen Set Solution, Najafgarh, Delhi
In this article

    A sheet roof in the Delhi sun turns the air under it into an oven, and the hot air has nowhere to go unless you give it a way out. The same roof can also leave the floor dim, so lights burn all day. Both are fixed by openings in the right places: low openings that let cool air in, high openings that let hot air out, and translucent panels that let daylight through.

    Our cooling guide covers insulation and the basics of turbo units. This article looks at the design side: how air actually moves, how to size openings, and how much roof to turn clear.

    How hot air really leaves a shed

    Hot air is lighter than cool air, so it rises and gathers under the roof. If there is an opening at the top, it escapes, and that pulls fresh air in somewhere lower down. This is the stack effect, and it needs no power. Wind adds to it by sucking air out over the ridge and pushing it in through openings facing the wind.

    The key point is that the air has to be able to come back in. A ventilator is not a vacuum cleaner that creates air from nothing. It moves air from one opening to another, so a roof outlet only works if there is an inlet low in the walls.

    Inlets: the half people forget

    A common failure is a shed with a row of turbo ventilators on the roof and tightly closed walls. The ventilators then pull air through door gaps and cracks, so very little moves. As a rule, the free opening area for inlets should be at least as large as the outlets, and it should sit low, around eave or wall level, so that fresh air sweeps across the floor before rising.

    • Eave openings: a gap under the eave or a louvre band just below it feeds the whole length evenly.
    • Wall louvres and open bays: good where wall height allows, with mesh to stop birds and dust.
    • Doors and shutters: a shutter left slightly open on a shaded side is a free inlet.

    In Delhi's pre-monsoon months the hot afternoon wind comes from the west. Big unshaded openings on that side can bring heat in. Put larger inlets on shaded or north and east faces, and keep west openings small or screened.

    Sizing the flow: air changes per hour

    Designers size ventilation by air changes per hour (ACH): how many times the whole volume of air under the roof is replaced in an hour. Higher ACH means cooler, cleaner air but more openings and more cost. The ranges below are planning figures, not code limits. Industrial processes with fumes follow the National Building Code and the factory rules.

    UsePlanning range (ACH)
    Dry storage, godown4 to 6
    Packing and light assembly6 to 8
    Workshop with machines8 to 12
    Welding, painting or heat processes15 to 20 or more, with local exhaust at the source

    For animal sheds the targets are very different and depend on the number and size of animals, so use our poultry and dairy shed guide.

    Daylight: how much roof should be clear

    A translucent sheet in the roof sends light down to the floor and lets you switch the lamps off in the day. But clear sheet also lets in solar heat and glare. Too little and the floor stays dim; too much and the shed heats up and workers squint. For most sheds the balance sits at about 5 to 10 percent of the roof area.

    Placement

    • Use the north slope. If the ridge runs east to west, roof lights on the north slope give soft, indirect light and take far less direct sun than the south slope in Delhi.
    • Spread them out. A few small panels spaced along the length light the floor more evenly than one big patch.
    • Keep them away from the eave edge. Put roof lights in the upper half of the slope, where they light the interior and not the walls.
    • Choose diffused sheet. Opal or translucent white gives less glare than crystal-clear sheet.

    Mistakes we see again and again

    1. Roof outlets, no inlets. Sealed walls turn the ventilators into decoration.
    2. Roof lights over workstations on the south slope. Direct sun and glare, then a screen or a blind is added later.
    3. Roof lights of a different profile. The laps do not match and the leaks start in the first monsoon.
    4. Ridge opening with no baffle. Rain blows straight in during storms.
    5. No maintenance. A turbo with a dry bearing stops spinning. Check that each head turns freely by hand every year before summer.
    6. Treating ventilation as a substitute for insulation. Do both: reflect and insulate first, then ventilate what remains.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many turbo ventilators does my shed need?

    Work backwards from the air you want to move. Multiply the shed volume by the air changes per hour for your use, then divide by what one unit moves in your wind. As a starting point many sheds end up with one 24 inch turbo for every 250 to 400 sq.ft of floor, but the estimator on this page lets you test your own figures.

    Does a turbo ventilator work when there is no wind?

    It works less well. A turbo ventilator is spun by wind, and it also lets rising hot air escape, so it gives some flow on still days. Both effects need replacement air to come in low down. If the shed is sealed, the turbo has nothing to pull.

    How much of the roof should be translucent?

    About 5 to 10 percent of the roof area is a common range for daylight without overheating. Go towards the lower end for west-facing roofs and heat-sensitive goods, and the higher end for workshops where good light matters more than heat.

    Is polycarbonate better than FRP for roof lights?

    UV-protected polycarbonate usually keeps its clarity and strength longer than ordinary FRP (fibreglass), which tends to yellow and turn brittle in strong sun. FRP costs less. Ask for written UV protection and a warranty period, and match the sheet profile to your metal sheet.

    Ridge ventilator or turbo ventilators: which is better?

    A ridge ventilator is a continuous opening along the ridge and suits long sheds, because it spreads the exit along the whole length and has no moving parts. Turbo units are easier to retrofit and work well when the roof has no ridge opening. Many industrial sheds use a ridge opening for large flows and add turbo units where heat collects.

    Will ventilation make the shed as cool as outside?

    It cannot go below the outside air temperature. What it removes is the extra heat that builds up under a sun-heated roof. Pair it with a light roof colour and insulation for the best result.
