Design Guide

Plan your shed layout: bays, columns and purlins

Before a contractor quotes, the frame has already been decided in outline: how many columns, how far apart, and how the roof sheets are held. Learn the words and try your own size.

November 202610 min readBy R.R Teen Set Solution, Najafgarh, Delhi
In this article

    Walk onto an empty plot and a shed looks like one big roof. To a fabricator it is a row of identical frames, joined along the length by purlins that carry the sheets. The number of frames, the distance between them and the distance between purlins are the three decisions that set the steel weight, the number of footings and, in the end, the price.

    This guide names each part, explains what changing it does, and lets you try your own size in a planner.

    The parts of a shed frame, one by one

    One slice through the building, taken across its width, is a frame. Every frame looks the same, and the building is just that slice repeated along the length.

    • Column: the vertical member that brings roof load to the ground. It stands on a base plate bolted to a concrete footing with anchor bolts.
    • Rafter or truss: the sloping member that spans from eave to ridge. Small sheds use rafters or a light truss; larger ones use a rolled or tapered section.
    • Purlin: a horizontal member laid across the rafters, along the length. The roof sheets are screwed to purlins, so the purlin spacing is the sheet's span.
    • Girt: the same idea on the wall. Wall sheets are fixed to girts running between columns.
    • Bracing: diagonals, usually in the roof and in one or two bays of the wall, that stop the whole building from racking sideways in the wind.
    • Eave and ridge: the low edge and the high line of the roof. Eave height is what counts for vehicles and stored goods.

    Bay spacing: fewer frames or lighter purlins

    A bay is the distance between two neighbouring frames along the length. You choose it, and it pushes cost in two opposite directions.

    Wide bays mean fewer frames, fewer columns and fewer footings. That saves digging, concrete and fabrication, and leaves more open floor. But purlins now span further, so they must be deeper and heavier. Narrow bays do the reverse: lighter purlins, but more frames and footings. Somewhere in between the total steel is at its lowest, and that is what a good structural design looks for.

    Shed typeUsual bayWhy
    Home shed, parking, terrace cover10 to 15 ftLight loads; small purlins are cheap and easy to handle
    Workshop, small factory15 to 20 ftBalances frame count against purlin size and keeps floor clear for machines
    Warehouse, PEB building20 to 30 ft (6 to 9 m)Large frames are costly, so fewer of them wins; bigger purlins are rolled by machine

    Do not choose the bay only by price. Match it to how the space will be used. Racking, vehicle bays and machine lines should line up with column positions, or the columns will sit in the middle of your work.

    Purlin spacing and roof slope

    Purlins sit along the slope at a fixed distance. With 0.45 to 0.50 mm profiled sheet this is commonly about 3.5 to 5 ft (roughly 1.1 to 1.5 m). Closer spacing allows thinner sheet and gives a firmer roof that drums less in rain. Wider spacing saves purlins but needs thicker or deeper-ribbed sheet, as explained in the sheet thickness guide.

    Slope is written as a ratio, for example 1 in 10 (rise of one for every ten run), which is about 6 degrees. Rain must leave the roof quickly and sheet laps must not let water creep backwards, so a slope below 1 in 10 is not advisable for most profiled sheeting. A steeper slope drains better and throws off dust, but it raises the ridge and the wall area, and it catches more wind.

    Eave height: set it from what goes inside

    What goes under the roofEave height to plan
    Cars and SUVs9 to 10 ft
    Small loading vehicles12 to 14 ft
    Trucks and containers15 to 16 ft
    Workshop with a chain pulley or hoist14 to 18 ft, more if the hoist needs lift height
    Racking and forklifts20 ft and above, measured to the underside of the lowest beam

    These are starting figures. Measure the tallest thing that will ever enter, add working clearance, and remember the roof sags slightly in the middle of a long span. The height under the lowest beam, not the eave, is the real limit.

    Wind: the load that pulls the roof off

    Steel sheet roofs are light, so downward weight is rarely their problem. Uplift is. Wind blowing across a roof creates suction, in the same way air flowing over an aeroplane wing does. On an open-sided or high-eave shed that suction can be larger than the roof's own weight, which means the frame must be held down as much as it is held up.

    The IS 875 (Part 3) wind map puts Delhi in the 47 m/s basic wind speed zone, which is about 170 km/h. Pre-monsoon storms in Delhi NCR regularly test sheds. This is why the anchor bolts, the base plate, the footing depth and the bracing are not places to save money. A shed fails at its connections far more often than in the steel sections themselves.

    Ask where the bracing is. If a drawing has no diagonals in the roof plane or in the walls, ask why. A frame without bracing may stand on a calm day and lean in the first storm.

    Five questions to settle before you ask for a quote

    1. What is the tallest thing that will enter, and where will it turn? This sets eave height and where columns can stand.
    2. Can you accept columns in the middle? A middle row cuts frame cost for wide sheds but costs floor space for ever.
    3. Where will doors and openings be? Doors are placed between frames, so bay lines should be planned around them.
    4. Will you extend later? Leave one gable end designed as an end frame that can be opened, and keep the plot free beyond it.
    5. What does the soil allow? Footing size and depth depend on the soil at your site, so a quote without a site visit is only a guess. Our construction checklist shows where this fits in the process.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a bay in a steel shed?

    A bay is the stretch of building between two neighbouring frames, measured along the length of the shed. A 80 ft long shed with frames every 20 ft has four bays and five frames.

    How far apart should the columns of a tin shed be?

    For small sheds, bay spacing of about 10 to 15 ft is common. For larger sheds and PEB frames, 20 to 30 ft (6 to 9 m) is typical because fewer footings and frames offset the heavier purlins. The right figure depends on roof load, wind and budget, so it is fixed in the structural design.

    What is the normal purlin spacing for roofing sheets?

    With 0.45 to 0.50 mm profiled sheet, purlins are commonly placed about 3.5 to 5 ft (roughly 1.1 to 1.5 m) apart along the slope. Thicker or deeper-ribbed sheet can span more. Ask the sheet maker for the load-span table.

    What roof slope is needed for a tin shed?

    Most profiled sheet roofs use a slope between about 1 in 10 and 1 in 6 (roughly 6 to 10 degrees). Very flat roofs leak at the laps and drain slowly, while steep ones cost more in frame and catch more wind.

    Can a shed have no columns in the middle?

    Yes. A clear-span frame carries the roof from outer wall to outer wall with no middle columns. For small and medium widths this is done with a truss or rafter frame. For very wide spans such as 60 ft or more, a PEB frame with tapered members is normally used.

    Does the planner give a final design?

    No. It counts frames, columns and purlin lines from the sizes you enter so you can discuss a layout and compare quotes. Member sizes, foundations and wind checks are done in the structural design for your site.
