Material Guide

Roofing sheet thickness and coating, decoded

A quote that says "26 gauge" tells you very little. Learn what thickness, zinc coating and paint each do, and check a delivery yourself with a few numbers and a weighing scale.

November 20269 min readBy R.R Teen Set Solution, Najafgarh, Delhi
In this article

    Two roofing sheets can both be called "26 gauge" and still differ by a fifth in steel weight, or by half in how long the zinc lasts. A sheet is described by three separate things: how thick the steel is, how much metal coating protects it, and what paint sits on top. Suppliers mix these up in conversation, and a quote that names only one of them hides the other two.

    This guide takes the three apart one at a time, shows what each costs you if you get it wrong, and gives you a calculator to check a delivery against its claimed spec.

    Gauge, mm, BMT and TCT: four words for "thickness"

    Gauge is an old sheet-metal numbering where a bigger number means thinner steel. The trouble is that several gauge charts exist, and they give different millimetres for the same number. In the Indian roofing trade, 28 gauge is usually taken as about 0.35 to 0.40 mm, 26 gauge as about 0.45 to 0.50 mm and 24 gauge as about 0.55 to 0.65 mm. Treat these as trade habit, not a standard.

    BMT (base metal thickness) is the steel only. TCT (total coated thickness) adds the metal coating and the paint on both faces, which is roughly 0.03 to 0.06 mm more on a painted sheet. A sheet sold as "0.50 mm TCT" can therefore contain only about 0.45 mm of steel. That 10 percent difference is the single most common surprise on site.

    Weight is useful because it is the one thing you can measure without special tools. Thinner steel weighs less, so if the weight per square metre is well below the table, the thickness is below what you were told.

    Always ask for millimetres in writing. A quote line should read like "0.50 mm BMT, Z150 coating, SMP paint" and not "26 gauge colour sheet".

    Coating: what GSM really measures

    Bare steel rusts. The metal coating, applied in a hot-dip line, is what stops that. Its weight is quoted in grams per square metre and the figure counts both faces together. Z120 therefore means 120 g/m² of zinc in total, or 60 g/m² on each face. Zinc has a density of about 7.14 g/cm³, so 60 g/m² works out to a layer roughly 8.4 micrometres thick on each side, less than a tenth of the thickness of a hair.

    Zinc protects in two ways. It forms a barrier, and where the steel is scratched or cut it corrodes first so the steel stays intact. That second effect is why a cut edge on a galvanised sheet does not rust at once. It is also why the zinc layer is slowly used up, and why its thickness sets a ceiling on life, whatever the paint looks like.

    Galvalume (AZ150): why it looks thicker on paper

    Galvalume carries an aluminium-zinc alloy coating, written AZ150 for 150 g/m². Because the alloy is lighter than zinc, that mass is a layer about 20 micrometres thick per side, more than double a Z120 sheet. Suppliers usually also claim it lasts several times longer than plain galvanising in the same air. For factories with process fumes, poultry and dairy sheds where ammonia is present, and sites close to heavy traffic or industry, it is the coating worth paying for. For a dry terrace cover, Z120 to Z150 with good paint is normally enough. The tin shed cost guide shows what galvalume adds per square foot.

    Weight alone cannot prove a coating class. For that you need the mill test certificate (MTC), which lists the coil number, the BMT and the coating class. Keep it with your invoice.

    Paint and colour: the layer people actually see

    On a colour-coated sheet the paint is a thin film, around 20 micrometres on the top face with a lighter backing coat underneath. It does three jobs: it slows the corrosion of the coating below, it carries the colour, and it decides how the sheet ages in the sun.

    Paint systemWhere it fitsWhat to watch
    RMP (regular modified polyester)Short-life or budget covers, mostly dry sitesFades and chalks sooner under strong sun
    SMP (silicone modified polyester)Most sheds, warehouses and terrace roofsGood all-round choice; cut edges still need sealing
    PVDFIndustrial air, long life, colour kept for many yearsCosts more; worth it only where the roof must look good for decades
    Plain GI (unpainted)Hidden or temporary workZinc is the only protection; it dulls and wears fastest

    Colour matters more than it seems. Sunlight is the main heat source for a metal roof, and a light colour reflects a large share of it. On a hot afternoon a dark roof surface can run 10 °C or more above a white one. If you are choosing a colour for a Delhi shed, pick the lightest tone that suits the building, then add insulation as described in our shed cooling guide.

    Profile: stiffness comes from shape, not only thickness

    A flat sheet bends under almost any load. Forming it into ribs makes it stiff, because the metal is moved away from the middle line, in the same way a folded sheet of paper stands up where a flat one flops. That is why a 0.45 mm profiled sheet can span a metre or more between purlins while the same steel left flat could not carry its own weight.

    The practical rule is that thickness, rib depth and purlin spacing are linked. A deeper rib or thicker sheet lets you place purlins further apart, which saves steel in the frame. A shallow sheet on wide purlins will sag, drum in the wind and leak at the laps. Ask the sheet maker for a load-span table, and read our shed layout guide to see how purlin spacing is planned.

    Formula: weight = (BMT × 7.85 × profile factor) + coating mass. Profile factors are approximate; your sheet maker's data sheet is final. Planning aid only, not a design or a warranty.

    Five checks when the sheets arrive

    1. Read the mill test certificate. It should show the coil number, BMT or TCT, and the coating class. The coil number is also stamped or printed on the sheet or bundle tag.
    2. Measure with a micrometer, not a tape. Take the reading on a flat part away from the rib and away from the cut edge, on several sheets from different bundles. Note whether you are reading steel only or the painted total.
    3. Weigh a counted bundle. Divide by the sheet count and the sheet area, then compare with the calculator above. A gap of more than a few percent is worth a written question.
    4. Look at the surface. White powdery stains mean the bundle got wet in storage. Scratches through the paint, dents on the ribs and mixed colour batches are easier to reject at the gate than after fixing.
    5. Store them right. Keep bundles off the ground, slightly inclined so water runs out, and covered. Never leave a wet bundle tightly packed for days.

    Frequently asked questions

    What thickness of roofing sheet is best for a tin shed?

    For most sheds and terrace covers in Delhi NCR, 0.45 to 0.50 mm is the usual choice. Go to 0.55 to 0.60 mm when purlins are far apart, when people will walk on the roof for maintenance, or when the shed is industrial. 0.35 to 0.40 mm suits light, short-life work such as temporary covers.

    Is 26 gauge the same as 0.5 mm?

    Not exactly. Different gauge charts give slightly different millimetres for the same gauge number, and in the Indian market 26 gauge is usually quoted between 0.45 and 0.50 mm. Ask for the thickness in millimetres, and ask whether it is base metal thickness (BMT) or total coated thickness (TCT).

    What does GSM mean on a roofing sheet?

    GSM here means grams per square metre of coating, counted on both faces together. A Z120 sheet carries 120 g/m² of zinc in total, which is about 60 g/m² per face or roughly 8 micrometres of zinc on each side.

    Is a higher GSM always better?

    A higher coating mass protects the steel for longer, but only if the sheet is also painted and installed well. A heavy zinc coat under a poor paint film, or cut edges left unsealed, will still fail early. Match the coating to the environment instead of buying the highest number.

    Does the colour of a roofing sheet change how hot the shed gets?

    Yes. Light colours reflect more sunlight, and a dark roof surface can run 10 °C or more hotter than a white one in direct summer sun. Colour does not replace insulation, but it is a free first step.

    How can I check the thickness of the sheet delivered to my site?

    Ask for the mill test certificate that shows coil number, thickness and coating class. Then measure a few sheets with a micrometer on the flat part (not on a rib crimp), and weigh a counted bundle. Compare the weight per square metre with the calculator in this article. A difference of more than a few percent is a reason to question the supplier.
